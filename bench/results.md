# Benchmark

`hashbench`, 3 repeats per cell. Generated by `scripts/run_benchmark.py`; raw runs in `results.csv`.

Submitting machine: Darwin · arm64 · Apple M4

> **Every peer here is another agent on the same physical machine.** They share
> one CPU, so there is no hardware speedup to measure and there never could be.
> What this table measures is **Haze's own overhead** — the cost of pairing,
> submitting, streaming progress and returning a result. Read the wall-vs-compute
> gap, not the ratios.

**compute** is what the worker reports. **wall** is what the person who submitted the job actually waits, including reaching the machine and getting the answer back. Medians.

| workload | local compute | local wall | desktop compute | desktop wall |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| small (40 rounds) | 0.45s | 0.47s | 0.40s | 0.96s |
| medium (200 rounds) | 2.12s | 2.21s | 2.01s | 2.54s |
| large (600 rounds) | 6.30s | 6.32s | 6.02s | 6.46s |

## What this shows

- **small**: 0.56s of Haze overhead on top of 0.40s of compute (58% of the round trip); 0.49× vs running locally
- **medium**: 0.53s of Haze overhead on top of 2.01s of compute (21% of the round trip); 0.87× vs running locally
- **large**: 0.44s of Haze overhead on top of 6.02s of compute (7% of the round trip); 0.98× vs running locally

Overhead is roughly **fixed** (about 0.44s here), so it dominates a short job and disappears into a long one. That is the whole argument for weighing transfer cost: below some size, offloading cannot win no matter how fast the other machine is, and a scheduler comparing raw speed would send the work anyway.

Because every peer here shares this machine's CPU, the ratios say nothing about hardware. Re-run with a genuinely separate machine to measure a speedup; the overhead figures above hold either way.
